In **August 2026**, owners of a smart pet feeder woke up to cats and dogs missing scheduled meals. The cause was not a jammed motor or an unplugged cord — it was an **outage of the feeder system's online connection**. Angry owners took to Reddit with a fair question: *why does my pet's food depend on an internet connection?*

It's the question every automatic-feeder buyer should ask before they spend a hundred-plus dollars on a "smart" model. Because the honest answer is: **some feeders genuinely need WiFi to work, and some do not.** Buying the wrong kind means your pet could be the one who goes hungry the morning your internet drops or a company's servers hiccup.

This guide explains the difference, which common feeders survive an outage, and the one test you should run at home before ever trusting any feeder alone with your pet.

## The short answer

**Most automatic pet feeders do not need WiFi to feed your pet — but the ones that do are exactly the ones most likely to fail during an outage.**

Two very different things get bundled under the label "automatic pet feeder":

- **Timed / offline feeders** store their feeding schedule **on the device**. A battery timer flips a motor at the programmed hour, no internet required. If every server in the world vanished, these keep working.
- **Smart / WiFi feeders** add an app, remote control, and often a camera. The schedule may live **on the device** (in which case feeding survives an outage, though you lose remote monitoring) **or in the cloud / depend on a live connection** (in which case an outage can stop meals).

The catch is you usually cannot tell which kind you're buying from the box. "App control," "WiFi enabled," and "smart" marketing language describes remote features — it does not tell you whether the *feeding itself* needs a connection. That distinction is the whole point of this guide.

## Did the August 2026 outage affect every feeder?

No. The outage hit owners of the affected **connected** system — the exact failure mode this article warns about. Owners of simple battery-timer feeders (which have no WiFi to begin with) noticed nothing at all. That asymmetry is the strongest argument for an offline feeder if you want zero dependence on a company's servers or your home network.

One nuance: several **smart** feeders store their schedule locally, so they *also* fed their pets during the outage — but their owners lost remote monitoring, alerts, and camera access until the connection returned, and they had **no way to check in remotely**. For a weekend away, "the food probably dispensed" is cold comfort when you cannot verify it.

The most outage-proof option is a **battery-powered timer feeder**: it has no WiFi to lose and keeps working through a power cut as long as batteries are fresh. The trade-off is that you give up remote monitoring. The middle option — a smart feeder that keeps the schedule on the device *and* has battery backup — is the best of both worlds in theory, but only if you verify it actually feeds offline.

## The one test you must run before any trip

No matter which type you own, spend **three days at home** test-feeding before you leave your pet alone with it. Here is the outage-specific version:

1. **Unplug the feeder** (or toggle its smart connection off in the app) while you are still home.
2. **Let it run your normal mealtimes** for a day or two.
3. **Watch or check whether your pet actually gets fed** on schedule the whole time.

If it feeds every meal while disconnected: the local timer works, and you can trust it for trips — subject to power backup. If meals stop the moment it loses its connection, you now know the truth, and you need a backup plan (a second offline feeder, or a neighbor or pet sitter on call) before you leave.

This one test would have spared the August 2026 owners a lot of panic, and it protects against any brand's outage — the ones we know about and the ones coming.

## How to choose for outage-proof feeding

Before buying, work through these four steps so you never discover the risk the hard way.

- **Ask "does scheduled feeding need a connection?" not "does it have WiFi?"** Read the specs or owner reviews specifically for whether meals dispense on a local timer when disconnected. If the manual says the schedule is stored "in the app" or "in the cloud," treat the feeder as connection-dependent until you test otherwise.
- **Prefer battery power for absolute fail-safety.** A battery-timer feeder (Cat Mate C500, and many WOPET/Cozila models) keeps working through both internet *and* power outages. A corded smart feeder needs battery backup at minimum — check it actually charges and holds.
- **Decide whether you're buying convenience or dependability.** If remote feeding and camera check-ins are essential, accept the outage trade-off and buy a model you've verified feeds offline. If dependability is the point, an offline timer is simpler and cheaper and cannot fail that way.
- **Keep a human or a second feeder as the ultimate backup.** No feeder is a substitute for a check-in on trips longer than a weekend. The fail-safe philosophy is "the feeder handles the routine; a person handles the plan."

## The bottom line

An automatic feeder is a convenience, and convenience should not become a single point of failure for your pet's meals. The August 2026 outage was a public reminder that "smart" and "connected" are features — not guarantees that your pet gets fed.

The fail-safe choice is simple: a **battery timer feeder has no WiFi and no cloud to go down**, and it is usually the cheaper option too. If you want cameras and app control, buy a smart feeder, but **verify it feeds offline before you trust it**, keep battery backup installed, and always have a person on call as the real backup.
