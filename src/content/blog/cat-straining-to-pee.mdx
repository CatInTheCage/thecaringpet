It usually starts late at night. Your cat goes to the litter box, squats, strains — and nothing happens. He goes back ten minutes later and tries again. He may cry out, lick at his rear end, or start squatting in odd places like the bathtub or a laundry basket. Owners describe it the same way every time: "he keeps trying to pee but nothing comes out."

Here is the one fact that matters more than everything else in this article: **if a cat is straining and producing no urine, he may be blocked — a urethral obstruction — and a blocked cat can die within 24 to 48 hours.** This is not a "wait until the vet opens" situation. It is a drop-everything, drive-now emergency. Male cats are affected far more often than females because their urethra is longer and narrower, and it is one of the few true emergencies in cats that can kill a young, otherwise healthy animal in two days.

This guide covers the 60-second check you can do tonight, how to tell a blockage from the less-deadly (but still urgent) conditions that mimic it, what actually happens at the emergency clinic, why the bill is what it is, and — if your cat is fine right now — the prevention steps that matter most.

## The 60-Second Check: Is Urine Coming Out?

Everything hinges on one question: **when your cat strains, does any urine come out?**

Go to the litter box with a flashlight. Rake through the litter. Look for recent wet spots, clumps, or dark spots on the floor, in the bathtub, on laundry. Then watch the next attempt.

- **No urine at all — or a few drops — over several attempts.** Treat this as a blockage until a vet says otherwise. Go now, even at 2 a.m. If you cannot tell, treat it as a blockage anyway; the downside of a false alarm is an exam fee, and the downside of guessing wrong is your cat's life.
- **Small amounts of urine, produced frequently, possibly with blood.** This is more often cystitis (bladder inflammation) — still needs a vet the same day, but it is not usually the clock-is-ticking emergency a blockage is.
- **Normal-sized puddles in inappropriate places.** More likely behavioral or litter-box-related, not a blockage.

One more sign that overrides everything above: a **hard, firm, painful belly**, especially if your cat growls or tenses when you gently touch it, or if he is hiding, vomiting, or refusing food. Those are late signs of a full bladder backing up into the kidneys. Stop reading and call an emergency clinic.

## The Red-Flag Signs of a Blocked Cat

A blocked cat rarely shows all of these. Any one of the first three, in a straining cat, is enough:

- Repeated trips to the litter box with little or no urine produced
- Crying or vocalizing while straining
- Licking the genital area repeatedly (not normal grooming — frantic, focused licking)
- A firm, distended, painful abdomen
- Vomiting, loss of appetite, or drooling (these appear as toxins build up)
- Hiding, lethargy, or unusual aggression when touched
- Squatting in strange places — bathtubs, sinks, piles of laundry

The progression has a grim rhythm owners describe again and again: frantic straining for hours, then the cat goes quiet and hides. That quiet phase is not improvement — it is a cat getting sicker. Potassium building up in the blood starts to affect the heart. From there, collapse and death can come quickly. Cornell Feline Health Center and every emergency-medicine source agree on the timeline: **a completely blocked cat typically survives 24 to 48 hours without relief, and sometimes less.**

## Why This Kills: What's Actually Happening

Understanding the mechanism makes the urgency obvious — and it explains why no home remedy can fix it.

The urethra — the tube from the bladder to the outside — plugs, most often with a soft mucus-and-crystal plug in male cats, sometimes with a small bladder stone. The bladder keeps filling, but there is nowhere for urine to go. The pressure backs up into the ureters and kidneys, which can no longer drain. The kidneys effectively shut down, and waste products that are normally excreted — including potassium — accumulate in the bloodstream.

Rising potassium is the killer: it slows and disrupts the heart's rhythm, which is why blocked cats sometimes die suddenly, before the bladder itself gives out. Meanwhile the bladder wall, stretched and deprived of blood flow, is at risk of rupture, and the retained urine begins to damage the kidneys themselves. Every hour of obstruction does damage that may or may not be fully reversible.

This is why "can't I just wait and see" is the one question with a hard no. There is no home technique that clears a urethral plug. Not water. Not a warm compress. Not massaging the bladder — which is actually dangerous, because you can rupture it. The only fix is a procedure at a veterinary clinic.

## Blocked vs. Cystitis vs. Behavioral: The Comparison Table

Urinary trouble in cats falls into three broad buckets. They overlap at the straining stage, which is why the urine check above matters so much:

| | **Urethral blockage** | **Cystitis / FIC** | **Behavioral / litter box issue** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Urine produced** | None, or a few drops | Small frequent amounts, often with blood | Normal amounts, in the wrong places |
| **Straining** | Constant, unproductive | Frequent, but productive | Occasional, casual posture |
| **Behavior** | Restless, then hiding; crying out; frantic licking | Uncomfortable but stays engaged; may avoid the box | Otherwise completely normal |
| **Timeline** | **Fatal in 24–48 hours** | Days; needs a vet within 24 hours | Not medical |
| **Most common in** | Male cats, often young, indoor, overweight | Cats under 10 (stress-linked FIC); older cats (infection is rarer than people think) | Any cat after a household change |
| **What to do** | **Emergency clinic now** | Same-day vet appointment | Look at the box and the household routine |

Two notes on the middle column, because it is where most straining cats actually land:

**FIC — feline idiopathic cystitis — is the most common cause of lower urinary signs in cats under 10**, accounting for roughly two-thirds of cases. "Idiopathic" means the bladder is inflamed without infection or stones; it is now understood to be stress-linked, flaring when a sensitive cat's environment changes (new pet, new schedule, a stray cat visible outside the window). The signs can look alarming — blood in the urine, straining, frequent trips — but urine is still being produced, and FIC usually resolves in days with pain control and stress management. The catch: a cat with FIC can still become blocked, and a blocked cat that unblocks can look "fine." The urine check is the only reliable divider.

**Urinary tract infection is uncommon in young cats.** When a vet finds bacteria in a young cat's urine, it is often secondary to another problem. In cats over 10, true infections become more common (often alongside kidney or thyroid disease) and need proper treatment. Either way, "my cat has a UTI, I'll get antibiotics" is a diagnosis that needs a urine test, not an assumption.

## What Happens at the Emergency Clinic

Knowing the sequence in advance makes the visit less scary — and makes the estimate easier to read.

1. **Stabilization.** The vet will palpate the bladder (a blocked bladder feels like a firm, tangerine-to-peach-sized organ), and likely run bloodwork immediately — specifically checking kidney values (BUN, creatinine) and potassium, because those determine how sick your cat really is.
2. **Decompressive cystocentesis.** If the bladder is dangerously full, the vet drains it with a needle through the abdominal wall. It sounds dramatic; it takes seconds, relieves the pressure, and can be lifesaving on its own.
3. **Unblocking.** Under sedation, a urinary catheter is passed up the urethra and the plug is flushed back into the bladder (retrograde urohydropropulsion). The bladder is emptied and flushed; the catheter usually stays in for 24–48 hours while the urethra recovers.
4. **Hospitalization.** Most blocked cats stay 1–3 days on IV fluids, which both rehydrate them and help the kidneys clear the backed-up waste. Repeated bloodwork confirms the potassium is normalizing.
5. **Going home.** With pain medication, sometimes a diet change, and a follow-up urine check. Some cats — roughly a quarter to a third — re-block within days or weeks, so the first two weeks at home are a watch period.

If a cat re-blocks repeatedly, the conversation turns to a surgery called a **perineal urethrostomy** — widening the urethral opening surgically. It is a bigger procedure with its own long-term considerations, but for cats that keep blocking, it is often the difference between a normal life and a fatal third episode.

## The Part Nobody Wants to Talk About: The Cost

A blocked cat is one of the most expensive common emergencies in feline medicine. In the United States, a typical unblocking with hospitalization runs **$1,000–$3,000** at an emergency clinic, depending on region, how sick the cat is on arrival, and how many days of hospitalization are needed. A perineal urethrostomy typically adds $2,000–$4,000.

That number is why you will find heartbreaking posts on every pet forum — owners facing a four-figure estimate at 3 a.m., sometimes for cats that were perfectly healthy two days earlier. If you are in that situation right now, three honest suggestions:

- **Ask the clinic what the estimate covers and what the tiers are.** There is often a difference between "what it takes to save him tonight" and the full gold-standard plan. Stabilization tonight plus transfer to your daytime vet in the morning can sometimes meaningfully lower the total.
- **Ask about payment options before treatment starts.** CareCredit and Scratchpay are accepted at most US emergency clinics. Many clinics also hold internal funds or know of local rescue organizations that help with emergency vet bills — the clinic staff deal with this question every night and most will help you look.
- **Do not skip the visit to save the exam fee.** A cat euthanized for cost that would have survived with 48 hours of care is the worst outcome in this entire article, and part of preventing it is knowing your options before you are standing at the front desk in shock.

For everyone else: this emergency is a large part of why pet insurance exists. A policy bought when your cat is young and healthy typically costs a fraction of one blockage episode, and urinary obstruction is squarely the kind of condition it covers.

## Prevention: What Actually Reduces the Risk

Blockages and FIC share enough risk factors that the prevention advice is the same, and it is genuinely effective — environmental change reduces FIC recurrence substantially in the research (the Ohio State College of Veterinary Medicine's "Indoor Pet Initiative" is the best-known framework). Four levers, in order of impact:

**1. Water, water, water.** Cats evolved from desert hunters with a weak thirst drive; a dry-food-only cat lives in a state of mild, chronic dehydration, and concentrated urine is the raw material for plugs and crystals. The single highest-impact change is shifting a meaningful portion of the diet to **wet food**, which is roughly 75% water. Water fountains help some cats drink more (many prefer moving water), and multiple water stations around the house mean drinking is never a trip worth skipping. ## What About Female Cats — and Dogs?

Female cats have a shorter, wider urethra, so obstruction is genuinely rare — but it happens, usually with a stone or tumor, and when it does it is just as much an emergency. A female cat straining unproductively gets the same advice: vet, now.

Dogs get their own version, most often from bladder stones, and certain breeds (Dalmatians, Bichons Frises, Miniature Schnauzers, Shih Tzus) are stone-prone. A dog straining to urinate with nothing coming out is the same emergency in a different body — go now. And if the straining came on right after eating something they shouldn't have, run it through our [toxin lookup tool](/tools/pet-toxin-lookup/) on the way to the phone.

## The Takeaway

If you take away three things from this article: **check for urine** — it is the divider between "emergency tonight" and "vet tomorrow." **Never wait out a blockage** — the mechanism is kidney pressure and heart-threatening potassium, and it kills inside two days. **And if your cat has recovered from one**, the prevention section is not optional reading — a cat that has blocked once is a cat likely to block again without the water and stress changes.
