If your older cat is losing weight, drinking from strange places, and turning up her nose at food, kidney disease is the first thing your vet will want to rule out. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is the most common serious condition of older cats — veterinary sources, including the Cornell Feline Health Center, commonly cite it in roughly **1 in 3 cats over 15** (some studies put it higher). That is one reason we recommend annual bloodwork for every cat past about age 10 — in human terms, using our 16-5-4 cat-age conversion, a 15-year-old cat is already around 73.

The encouraging news: CKD is not a death sentence. Caught at an early stage and managed well, many cats live comfortable, happy lives for **years** after diagnosis. This guide covers how the feline kidney fails, the early signs owners actually notice at home, what the vet will do, and the day-to-day management that matters most — including the one situation that never waits: a cat who has stopped eating.

> **This article is educational, not a substitute for veterinary care.** Kidney values are interpretable only alongside a urinalysis and an exam. If your cat is not eating, is vomiting repeatedly, or seems weak, call a vet today — don't wait to finish reading.

## What Kidney Disease Actually Does to a Cat

The kidneys filter waste from the blood, concentrate urine so the body keeps its water, regulate potassium and phosphorus, help control blood pressure, and signal the bone marrow to make red blood cells. CKD means kidney tissue has been slowly, irreversibly lost until there aren't enough working nephrons left to do these jobs.

Two details explain almost everything you'll observe at home:

1. **Lost concentrating ability comes first.** A healthy cat's kidneys are so efficient that a cat on dry food may barely drink at all. As CKD develops, the kidneys can no longer concentrate urine, so the cat must drink enormously to keep up — and urinate enormously to match. That is why the classic early picture is a skinny old cat who is *always* at the water bowl and flooding the litter box.
2. **Waste products accumulate quietly.** Urea and other metabolites that healthy kidneys excrete build up in the blood. This is what causes the vague late signs — nausea, poor appetite, vomiting, dull coat, and the distinctive ammonia-like "uremic" breath.

CKD is **chronic and progressive**, but it usually progresses slowly, in months-to-years steps rather than days. That slowness is your opportunity: the earlier it is caught, the more of that runway you have to work with.

## Early Signs of Kidney Disease in Cats (What Owners Notice First)

Cats are masters at hiding illness, and CKD's earliest signs are easy to dismiss as "just getting old." The pattern below — several mild changes appearing together over months — is what should prompt a vet visit and a senior blood panel:

| Sign | What it looks like at home | Why it happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Drinking and urinating more** | Emptying the water bowl, "clumping" litter into huge wet clumps, accidents outside the box | Kidneys can no longer concentrate urine |
| **Weight loss** | Spine and hips feel sharper; a formerly picky-over-nothing cat gets bony | Muscle loss and poor appetite from accumulating waste |
| **Reduced appetite, pickiness** | Eats a few bites, walks away; prefers strong-smelling food | Nausea from uremic toxins |
| **Dull, unkempt coat** | Greasy fur, less grooming | Nausea, dehydration, feeling unwell |
| **Vomiting or hairballs** | More frequent than the cat's lifelong baseline | Uremic irritation of the stomach |
| **Lethargy, sleeping more** | Skips play, hides more | Anemia and general toxicity |
| **Bad breath** | Ammonia- or urine-like smell | Urea broken down to ammonia in saliva |

One sign alone — say, a slightly pickier appetite in a 12-year-old — is worth mentioning at the next visit. **Three or more of these together, or any of them plus weight loss, warrants a blood panel now.**

Also note what is *not* on this list: most cats with early CKD do **not** look obviously sick. They adapt. Waiting for a cat to "look sick" means catching the disease one or two stages later than you could have.

## The Vet Visit: Diagnosis and Staging

Diagnosis is straightforward; interpreting it well is where a good vet earns their keep.

**Bloodwork** checks BUN and creatinine (waste levels), the newer **SDMA** marker (which flags kidney loss earlier than creatinine alone), phosphorus, potassium, and red blood cell count. **Urinalysis** checks how concentrated the urine is — in CKD it is typically dilute — and looks for protein loss. **Blood pressure** is measured because kidney disease and hypertension feed each other, and hypertension itself can damage the eyes and brain. **Thyroid testing** matters too, because hyperthyroidism is the other great mimic of the "old, thin, ravenous cat," and the two conditions frequently overlap.

If kidney values are elevated, vets stage CKD using the **IRIS system (stages 1–4)**, based mainly on creatinine and SDMA, with substages for blood pressure and protein in the urine. Staging is not about labeling a cat — it drives treatment:

- **Stage 1** (kidneys losing function, values still normal): usually no treatment yet, but monitoring begins.
- **Stage 2** (early, values mildly elevated): this is the sweet spot — dietary change and monitoring make the biggest difference. Published survival studies of IRIS-staged cats put median survival at roughly **3 years** at this stage.
- **Stage 3** (moderate): medication, renal diet, sometimes subcutaneous fluids at home. The same studies put median survival at around **1–2 years** with treatment.
- **Stage 4** (advanced): supportive care focused on quality of life; survival is usually measured in weeks to months, though some cats beat the averages considerably.

These medians come from studies, not promises — individual cats vary enormously, and treatment moves the numbers.

Wet food is roughly 75% water versus ~10% for kibble, and many vets now recommend transitioning CKD cats to an all-wet or mostly-wet diet for exactly this reason. **2. Renal (kidney-support) diet.** Prescription renal diets restrict phosphorus and use specific protein levels and added omega-3s. This is the one intervention with the strongest evidence behind it — studies show renal diets meaningfully extend survival in stages 2–3. These foods require a prescription, and they only work if the cat eats them — which leads to the next point.

**3. Treating nausea and appetite.** Poor appetite in CKD is usually nausea, not pickiness. Anti-nausea medication, appetite stimulants, and sometimes B12 (cobalamin) supplementation turn "barely eating" back into "eating normally" for many cats.

**4. Phosphorus control.** High phosphorus speeds kidney damage. If diet alone doesn't bring levels down, vets add phosphate binders — powders or gels mixed into food.

**5. Potassium, blood pressure, anemia.** Low potassium causes weakness; supplementing fixes it. Hypertension is managed with medication. Anemia from reduced erythropoietin production can be treated with injections that stimulate red blood cell production in more advanced cases.

**6. Subcutaneous (sub-Q) fluids at home.** For moderate-to-advanced CKD, many owners learn to give warmed fluids under the skin — a 5–10 minute routine, usually every few days — which flushes toxins and is one of the most quality-of-life-improving interventions in feline medicine. It looks intimidating; most owners report it quickly becomes unremarkable.

## When a CKD Cat Stops Eating: This Is the Emergency

Here is the rule every cat owner should know, and it matters double for a cat with kidney disease: **a cat that has eaten nothing for 24 hours needs a vet the same day.**

Cats are not dogs. When a cat stops eating — from nausea, a flare of disease, or anything else — the body starts mobilizing fat to the liver for energy, and the feline liver is easily overwhelmed. Fat accumulates inside liver cells, the liver starts failing, and the cat, now nauseous because of the liver, eats even less. This condition, **hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver)**, can be fatal on its own, and it is far more likely in overweight cats. What started as "she's off her food" becomes a feeding-tube hospitalization within a week or two.

For a CKD cat specifically, a sudden appetite crash can also signal:

- a **crash** (acute worsening — often triggered by dehydration, a urinary issue, or an infection) that IV fluids can reverse,
- a **urinary blockage** (if your cat is also straining or producing no urine, this is a same-hour emergency — read [our guide to straining and blockage](/blog/cat-straining-to-pee/)),
- or simply uncontrolled nausea that a changed medication plan can fix.

None of these are things to watch and wait on. The paradox of CKD management is that a chronic, months-long disease still produces genuine same-day emergencies — appetite is the tripwire.

## Day-to-Day Life With a CKD Cat

Owners of diagnosed cats consistently describe the same routine, and it is far more ordinary than the diagnosis sounds:

- **Feed the renal diet, but prioritize eating.** A cat that eats the "wrong" food beats a cat that eats nothing. Any diet change is gradual, over 1–2 weeks.
- **Weigh weekly.** A kitchen scale with the cat in a carrier, or a baby scale, catches decline weeks before you can feel it. A consistent downward trend = call the vet.
- **Track water and litter.** Bigger clumps, a drier water fountain — or suddenly smaller clumps and a full fountain — both tell you something.
- **Keep up dental care and stress control.** Dental infections seed the bloodstream and stress spikes blood pressure; both tax kidneys.
- **Recheck on schedule.** Stage 2 cats typically recheck bloodwork every 3–6 months, stage 3 every 1–3 months. These visits are how treatment stays matched to the disease.

And keep perspective on the calendar. A cat diagnosed at stage 2, managed well, often has **years** of sunbeams, lap time, and 4 a.m. yelling left. The goal of CKD care is not to defeat a chronic disease — it is to make the years it allows genuinely good ones. ## The Bottom Line

Kidney disease is the most common serious diagnosis in senior cats, and the deck is stacked in favor of owners who pay attention early. Watch the water bowl and the litter box, feel for the spine and hips monthly, weigh your cat now and then, and blood-test every senior cat yearly — twice-yearly past 12. If the diagnosis comes, treat the nausea, feed the prescription diet, keep the water flowing, and recheck on schedule. And remember the one rule that overrides everything else in this article: **in a cat, not eating for 24 hours is a same-day vet visit — no exceptions, especially with kidney disease.**
