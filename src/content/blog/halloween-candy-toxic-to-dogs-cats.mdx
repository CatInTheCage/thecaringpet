Halloween is the single most candy-dense night of the year in your home. Bowls of chocolate sit at kid-height by the front door. Treat bags are dumped on living-room floors and picked through by toddlers who drop half of what they grab. Gum and sugar-free candy ride in coat pockets at exactly nose height for a dog. And the doorbell rings every ninety seconds, which means your pet is loose, excited, and unsupervised in the exact room where all of this is happening.

It is not a theoretical risk. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center lists chocolate and candy as the top pet poison call drivers around Halloween, and the pattern repeats every year: a large-breed dog eats a bowlful of candy and is fine, while a ten-pound terrier gets seriously ill from three pieces of dark chocolate. The difference is not luck — it is the type of candy, the amount, and the weight of the animal.

This guide ranks the Halloween dangers by how likely they are to actually hurt your pet, gives you the weight-and-amount math for chocolate (the one toxin you can roughly calculate at home), and walks through exactly what to do in the first thirty minutes after you discover a shredded wrapper.

> **If your pet has already eaten candy** — call a veterinarian or a poison hotline now, before symptoms start. In the U.S., the [ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control) (888-426-4435) and [Pet Poison Helpline](https://www.petpoisonhelpline.com/) (855-764-7661) are staffed 24/7, even on Halloween night. If you are not sure whether what they ate counts as an emergency, our [Pet Poison Triage wizard](/tools/pet-poison-triage/) sorts red-flag symptoms in under a minute.

## The Quick-Reference Danger Table

Before the details, here is the ranking. "Fast" means symptoms can begin within the hour — those are the ones where you call first and read this article second.

| Halloween item | Toxic ingredient | Danger level | How fast it hits | First move |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dark / baking chocolate | Theobromine + caffeine | Severe | 2–12 hours | Call vet or hotline immediately |
| Sugar-free gum & candy | Xylitol | Severe (dogs) | 30–60 minutes | Call vet or hotline immediately |
| Chocolate-covered raisins | Theobromine **and** a kidney toxin | Severe (double hazard) | Hours | Call vet or hotline immediately |
| Any raisins / grape candy | Unknown kidney toxin | Severe (dogs) | Hours | Call vet or hotline immediately |
| Cannabis edibles | THC | Severe | 30 min–3 hours | Call vet — be honest about what it was |
| Candy wrappers & foil | Physical obstruction | Moderate to severe | Hours to days | Call vet if vomiting or no appetite |
| Macadamia-nut chocolates | Unknown toxin + theobromine | Moderate to severe | 6–24 hours | Call vet or hotline |
| Candy corn, hard candy | Sugar only | Low (GI upset in quantity) | Hours | Watch; call if vomiting persists |
| Caramel apples | Sugar, sticks | Low to moderate | — | Watch for swallowed stick; call if gagging |
| Glow sticks / glow jewelry | Phenol (bitter liquid) | Low (cats) | Minutes (drooling) | Rinse mouth; usually self-resolving |

One pattern worth internalizing: the deadliest items on this list are not the ones that look scary. Candy corn — pure sugar — is nearly harmless. The killers are the small, innocent-looking things: two pieces of 90% dark chocolate, a pack of sugar-free gum, a mini-box of raisins.

## Chocolate: The One You Can Actually Calculate

Chocolate is the classic Halloween emergency, and the only one where a rough at-home calculation is possible. The toxic principle is **theobromine**, a caffeine relative that dogs and cats metabolize far more slowly than we do. It accumulates, overstimulates the heart and nervous system, and in serious cases causes tremors, seizures, and fatal heart arrhythmias. The full species-by-species breakdown is in our [Chocolate toxin entry](/tools/pet-toxin-lookup/chocolate/).

Three variables decide whether a chocolate exposure is an emergency: **the darkness of the chocolate, the amount eaten, and your pet's weight.**

Theobromine content by chocolate type (approximate, per ounce / 28 g, per the Merck Veterinary Manual):

| Chocolate type | Theobromine per oz | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| White chocolate | ~0.25 mg | Theobromine-free in practice; mostly a fat/sugar stomach risk |
| Milk chocolate | ~44–58 mg | Dangerous in larger amounts, especially for small dogs |
| Dark / semi-sweet (50–70%) | ~130–160 mg | A few pieces can sicken a small dog |
| Baking chocolate, cocoa powder | ~400–800 mg | A single square can be an emergency for a dog under 20 lb |

The thresholds veterinary toxicology uses: **mild signs (vomiting, restlessness, a racing heart) begin around 20 mg of theobromine per kg of body weight**, and **tremors and seizures become possible around 40–50 mg/kg**.

Put that in real terms:

- **A 75 lb Labrador eats the whole bowl of milk chocolate Snickers minis (say 10 oz of chocolate).** He ingests roughly 500 mg of theobromine against 34 kg of body weight — about 15 mg/kg. Likely outcome: vomiting, diarrhea, one miserable night, and a vet call anyway to confirm.
- **A 12 lb terrier eats two squares of 70% dark chocolate (about 1 oz).** That is roughly 150 mg against 5.5 kg — about 27 mg/kg, past the symptom threshold for a dog a quarter her size. That is a same-hour call to the vet or poison hotline.

The rule of thumb: **severe poisoning territory for milk chocolate starts around one ounce per pound of body weight — and dark chocolate gets there at roughly a tenth of that.** When in doubt, treat it as dark chocolate and call.

Symptoms typically start within 2 to 12 hours: vomiting, diarrhea, restlessness, panting, a racing heart, then tremors. Do not wait for symptoms to appear before calling — decontamination (inducing vomiting, activated charcoal) works best early, and it must be done under veterinary guidance.

## Xylitol: The Most Dangerous Thing in a Coat Pocket

If chocolate is the best-known Halloween hazard, **xylitol is the most underestimated one**. It is a sugar substitute used in sugar-free gum, mints, and increasingly in "healthy" and keto candy — the exact candies adults hand out so kids' parents "won't hate them." To a dog, it is genuinely dangerous.

In dogs, xylitol triggers a massive insulin release. **Blood sugar can crash within 30 to 60 minutes** of eating even a small amount — the commonly cited danger threshold is on the order of 0.1 gram per kg of body weight, which a single piece of some gum brands can approach for a small dog. Weakness, staggering, and seizures follow. Higher doses can then cause **acute liver failure** over the following hours to days. Our full [Xylitol toxin entry](/tools/pet-toxin-lookup/xylitol/) covers the mechanism and the product list.

Two things make xylitol exposures treacherous:

1. **The window is short.** A dog can go from normal to seizing inside an hour. If you find a chewed pack of sugar-free gum, call before you clean up.
2. **Do not induce vomiting on your own.** Unlike chocolate, making a xylitol dog vomit without professional guidance can drop blood sugar faster. Call first; the hotline or vet will tell you what to do, and may have you rub honey or corn syrup on the gums if hypoglycemia starts before you reach them.

Cats appear somewhat less sensitive to xylitol than dogs, but it is still considered risky for them — the answer to "my cat ate sugar-free gum" is the same phone call.

## Chocolate-Covered Raisins: The Double Hazard

The mini-box of raisins — the treat every kid trades away — deserves special mention because it combines two severe toxins in one piece. Chocolate brings theobromine; raisins bring an as-yet-unidentified toxin that can cause **acute kidney failure in dogs**.

The raisin problem is that the dose-response is wildly unpredictable. Some dogs eat a pound of grapes and are fine; others develop kidney failure from a small handful. Veterinary toxicology has never isolated the toxin or a safe threshold, which is why the standing guidance is blunt: **any ingestion of grapes or raisins by a dog warrants a call**, with no "wait and see" period. Early signs are vomiting (often within hours), then lethargy, increased thirst, and decreased urination as the kidneys struggle. The full profile is in our [Grapes & Raisins toxin entry](/tools/pet-toxin-lookup/grapes-raisins/).

So a chocolate-covered raisin is simultaneously a chocolate exposure and a grape exposure — and the two have different treatments and timelines. If your dog eats them, tell the hotline or vet both facts, not just "he ate chocolate."

## The Overlooked Hazards

### Candy wrappers and foil

A dog who raids a treat bowl swallows the packaging along with the candy. Individually, foil and cellophane are usually too small and crumpled to obstruct — the stomach acid does the work on the candy and the wrappers often pass. The risk scales with quantity: a dog who ate the entire bowl, wrappers and all, can develop a **partial intestinal obstruction**. Signs appear over hours to days: repeated vomiting, refusing food, lethargy, no stool. This is a vet visit, not a hotline call, but it is a real one — and an expensive one if it ends in surgery.

### Cannabis edibles

In states where cannabis is legal, veterinary poison lines report a steady year-over-year rise in THC exposures, and Halloween — with brownies, gummies, and chocolates sitting in the same bowl as everything else — is a recognized spike night. THC causes lethargy, wobbliness, dribbling urine, low heart rate, and in large doses, incoordination bad enough that a dog injures itself. It is rarely fatal but can require supportive care.

If this happens, **tell the vet exactly what it was.** Veterinarians are not law enforcement; they need the real substance to treat correctly, and "he ate something, we don't know what" is the single most unhelpful sentence in emergency veterinary medicine.

### Caramel apples and the rest of the party food

The apple itself is harmless. The risks are the wooden stick (a swallowing/choking hazard that shows up on X-rays every November), the sugar and fat (a stomach upset, or pancreatitis in sensitive dogs from the caramel), and anything the caramel is rolled in — chocolate-covered nuts being the worst, since macadamias are their own toxin. Check our [Macadamia Nuts entry](/tools/pet-toxin-lookup/macadamia-nuts/) for that one.

### Pumpkin spice and adult drinks

Pumpkin-spice everything is mostly a non-issue — the amount of nutmeg in a latte or baked good is far below toxic levels. Only if a dog gets into the spice jar itself (large amounts of nutmeg can cause disorientation and tremors) does it matter; details in the [Nutmeg entry](/tools/pet-toxin-lookup/nutmeg/). Alcohol at Halloween parties is a more realistic exposure — dogs lap abandoned cups, and ethanol hits small bodies hard. See the [Alcohol entry](/tools/pet-toxin-lookup/alcohol/) for signs and thresholds.

### Glow sticks and glow jewelry (the cat problem)

Cats are the species most likely to bite into a glow stick, and the liquid inside — a phenol compound with an intensely bitter taste — causes immediate dramatic drooling, gagging, and mouth-pawing. It looks alarming and is almost never serious: the taste is self-limiting, and the standard response is to rinse the mouth, offer water or a bit of wet food, and keep the cat somewhere dim and quiet. The one situation to escalate: if the plastic itself was swallowed, or if signs last beyond a few hours. Full write-up in our [Glow Jewelry entry](/tools/pet-toxin-lookup/glow-jewelry/).

## Cats and Halloween Candy: Usually a Non-Event — With Exceptions

Cats are picky. Most want nothing to do with candy — they lack the sweet-taste receptor that drives dogs (and kids) to the bowl. The typical feline Halloween emergency is not candy at all but **stress**: the doorbell, the strangers, the noise. A stressed cat bolts out the front door — Halloween and July 4th are the two classic lost-cat nights — or hides somewhere unsafe.

The exceptions worth knowing:

- **Chocolate is more dangerous per pound for cats** than dogs; they are very sensitive to theobromine. Any chocolate eaten by a cat is worth a call, at any amount.
- **Glow sticks** (above) are the actual cat-specific Halloween toxin.
- **Decorations** — stringy fake cobwebs, ribbon, battery-operated tea lights — present chew and swallow hazards. String foreign bodies are surgical emergencies in cats, and button batteries are a chemical burn risk (see [Batteries](/tools/pet-toxin-lookup/batteries/)).

The fix for cats is simple: **one closed room, set up before trick-or-treat starts, with the cat inside it.** Food, water, a litter box, a hiding spot. It beats a $4,000 search for a cat two blocks away wearing a costume.

## What to Do in the First 30 Minutes

If you come back into the room and find a torn treat bag, work in this order:

1. **Secure the scene.** Remove all remaining candy from reach, and move your pet away from the mess so they cannot eat more while you assess.
2. **Collect the evidence.** Grab the wrapper(s), the bag with the ingredient list, and count roughly how many pieces are missing. For chocolate: note the type and cocoa percentage. For anything sugar-free: photograph the ingredient list — the word you are looking for is **xylitol**, sometimes labeled "birch sugar."
3. **Call a vet or poison hotline before symptoms appear.** ASPCA (888-426-4435) or Pet Poison Helpline (855-764-7661), or your local emergency vet. Answer their questions straight, including the cannabis question if that is what happened. They will tell you whether to come in, whether to induce vomiting, or whether watchful waiting is genuinely safe.
4. **Do not improvise.** No home remedies, no salt, no hydrogen peroxide unless a professional tells you to give it — and specifically not for xylitol exposures.
5. **If you are told to monitor:** normal water, normal (or slightly delayed) dinner, and a written log of the time of ingestion, what you found, and every symptom with a timestamp. Re-check your pet every couple of hours overnight.

Not sure the situation even qualifies? Run the red-flag checklist in our [Pet Poison Triage wizard](/tools/pet-poison-triage/) — it covers the symptoms (vomiting, tremors, staggering, collapse) that mean skip the waiting and go.

## Prevention: How to Get Through the Night

The engineering answer to Halloween is to make the candy physically inaccessible and your pet mentally occupied. The first part is positioning: candy bowl elevated or covered, treat bags on top of the fridge (not the counter), coats with gum in them hung up rather than draped over a chair at dog level. The second part is giving the dog something better to do during the two peak hours — which is exactly what long-lasting food toys are for. ## The Night-Of Checklist

- Candy bowl elevated and covered; backup supply in a closed cabinet
- Treat bags and coats with gum physically out of reach, not just "on the counter"
- Dog walked and fed before trick-or-treat hours (a tired, fed dog raids less)
- Frozen stuffed toy ready to deploy at first doorbell
- Cat in a closed room with food, water, litter, and a hiding spot — door closed before the first knock
- Glow sticks and fake cobwebs up high or skipped entirely
- Both hotline numbers in your phone *before* the night: ASPCA 888-426-4435, Pet Poison Helpline 855-764-7661
- Pet wearing ID (and a tracking tag if they have one) all night, in case of a door dash

## The Bottom Line

Rank the Halloween risks by what actually sends pets to the ER: **dark chocolate and sugar-free gum first, raisins close behind, cannabis edibles rising every year, wrappers as the sleeper, and candy corn as a stomachache at worst.** The protection is unglamorous — elevation, a closed door, a frozen rubber toy, and two phone numbers saved in advance — and it is nearly all of it. The pets who get hurt on Halloween are almost never the ones with careful owners who know the list; they are the ones nobody thought would reach the bowl.
